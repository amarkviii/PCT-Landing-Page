Pool chlorine levels: FC, TC, and CC explained

5 min read · Updated June 17, 2026
Free chlorine (FC) is the active sanitizer. Normal is 2–4 ppm without CYA — but rises as CYA increases.
Combined chlorine (CC) causes pool smell and irritation. Keep CC below 0.5 ppm regardless of CYA.

Normal pool free chlorine (FC) is 2–4 ppm — but only when cyanuric acid (CYA) is low or absent. Every threshold shifts as CYA rises: at CYA 80, 8 ppm FC is squarely in the target range and safe to swim in. This page starts with the no-CYA baseline, then covers CYA-adjusted targets that apply to most real outdoor pools. Combined chlorine (CC) should stay below 0.5 ppm regardless of CYA.

Free chlorine (FC) levels — baseline (no CYA)

Free chlorine is the active sanitizer in your pool — the chlorine that's available to kill bacteria and algae. This is the most important number on your test. The table below applies to pools with little or no cyanuric acid (CYA under 20 ppm) — a freshly filled pool, an indoor pool, or one without any stabilizer added. If your pool has CYA, use the FC/CYA table further down instead.

FC Level (ppm)StatusAction
0No sanitizerAdd chlorine immediately. Your pool has no protection against bacteria or algae
0.1 – 0.9Dangerously lowAdd chlorine now. Water is not safe for swimming
1.0 – 1.9LowAcceptable short-term, but raise to 2+ ppm soon
2.0 – 4.0IdealNo action needed. This is your target range
4.1 – 7.9HighLet it drop naturally. Avoid swimming above 5 ppm (at low CYA — threshold rises with CYA; see table below)
8.0+Too highDo not swim at low CYA. Wait for FC to drop to a safe level. At CYA 50+, safe FC is higher — check the FC/CYA table below before acting

How CYA changes your FC target

Most outdoor pools contain cyanuric acid (CYA) — either from stabilized chlorine tablets or direct CYA addition. Once CYA is present, the baseline table above no longer applies. Your safe FC minimum scales with CYA: at CYA 30 the minimum is about 2.25 ppm; at CYA 50 it's about 4 ppm; at CYA 80 it's about 6 ppm. Saltwater/SWG pools use a lower ratio (CYA × 5%) because the salt cell continuously replenishes consumed chlorine.

Combined chlorine (CC) levels

Combined chlorine is chlorine that's already reacted with contaminants — it's spent and no longer sanitizing. You calculate it by subtracting FC from TC.

Combined Chlorine = Total Chlorine − Free Chlorine
CC Level (ppm)StatusAction
0 – 0.2ExcellentNo action needed
0.2 – 0.5NormalAcceptable. Monitor on next test
0.5 – 1.0ElevatedConsider shocking soon if this persists
1.0+HighShock the pool. Chloramines are building up

High combined chlorine is what causes "that pool smell." It also irritates eyes and skin. If CC stays above 0.5 ppm across multiple tests, it's time to shock.

Total chlorine (TC) levels

Total chlorine is simply FC + CC. On its own, TC doesn't tell you much — what matters is how close it is to your free chlorine reading.

ScenarioWhat It MeansAction
TC equals FCNo combined chlorine. All your chlorine is activeNothing — this is perfect
TC slightly above FCSmall amount of CC. Normal after heavy useMonitor. Shock if it persists
TC much higher than FCHigh CC. Chloramines are building upShock immediately

When to test chlorine

  • Routine: 2–3 times per week during swim season
  • After heavy use: Pool parties, lots of swimmers, heavy rain
  • After shocking: Test the next day to confirm FC has dropped to safe levels
  • After adding chemicals: Wait at least 30 minutes with the pump running, then retest
  • Time of day: Morning gives the most accurate baseline — UV destroys chlorine throughout the day. Full testing schedule →

Frequently asked questions

What is the normal (ideal) chlorine level for a pool?

With little or no CYA, 2 to 4 ppm free chlorine is the target and combined chlorine should stay below 0.5 ppm. Once CYA is present — as it is in most outdoor pools — those thresholds rise with your stabilizer level. See the FC/CYA chart for exact targets at every CYA level.

What chlorine level is too high or too low for a pool?

Too low: Without CYA, below 2 ppm is under-sanitized and below 1 ppm is unsafe to swim in. With CYA present, "too low" rises with your stabilizer level — always check FC against your CYA, not as a standalone number.

Too high: Without CYA, above 5 ppm is uncomfortable and above 8 ppm you should wait before swimming. With higher CYA, the safe upper limit is proportionally higher. Combined chlorine (CC) above 0.5 ppm means chloramines are building up regardless of FC level — that's usually the bigger problem.

What is the chlorine level for a saltwater pool?

Saltwater pools target the same free chlorine range as traditional chlorine pools — 2 to 4 ppm in low-CYA water. But salt water generators typically run with CYA at 60 to 80 ppm to protect the generated chlorine from sunlight, which pushes the FC target up to 4 to 6 ppm.

The chlorine itself behaves identically; only the source (salt cell vs liquid or tablets) is different. See saltwater vs chlorine pools for the broader comparison.

Common chlorine problems

Why is total chlorine higher than free chlorine?

When TC is noticeably higher than FC, it means combined chlorine (chloramines) are building up in your water. Combined chlorine is spent — it's already reacted with sweat, urine, sunscreen, or other organics and is no longer sanitizing.

A small gap (0.2 ppm or less) is normal. But if TC is 0.5 ppm or more above FC, chloramines are accumulating and you need to shock. Superchlorination breaks apart chloramines and resets your water. See Free Chlorine vs Total Chlorine for the full breakdown.

Why is my free chlorine low?

Free chlorine drops for a handful of predictable reasons:

  • Sunlight without CYA: UV radiation destroys unprotected chlorine in hours. If your CYA is too low, FC won't hold
  • CYA too high: Paradoxically, excess stabilizer locks up chlorine and makes it ineffective — your test reads "low" because less FC is available to sanitize
  • Algae growth: Even an early bloom you can't see yet will consume chlorine rapidly. If FC drops faster than expected, check for signs of algae
  • Heavy bather load: Swimmers introduce organics that react with and consume FC
  • Chloramine buildup: If CC is high, some of your chlorine supply is being converted to useless combined chlorine instead of staying active

The fix depends on the cause. Start by testing CYA alongside FC — the FC/CYA chart tells you whether your FC is truly low or just appears low for your stabilizer level. If you need to raise it, see How to Raise Pool Chlorine Safely.

Why does my pool smell like chlorine?

A strong "chlorine smell" almost never means there's too much chlorine. It means there's too much combined chlorine. Chloramines — the byproduct of chlorine reacting with nitrogen-based contaminants — are what produce that harsh chemical odor and irritate eyes and skin.

The fix is counterintuitive: add more chlorine. Shocking the pool to breakpoint chlorination destroys chloramines and eliminates the smell. If CC is above 0.5 ppm on your test, that confirms the diagnosis. A properly chlorinated pool with low CC should have almost no smell at all.

Do pool chlorine levels change with water temperature?

Yes — warmer water demands more chlorine. Higher temperatures speed up bacteria and algae growth, increase the rate of chemical reactions that consume FC, and accelerate chlorine off-gassing. What's sufficient at 70°F may not be enough at 90°F.

As a rule of thumb: if your water temperature climbs above 84°F (29°C), test FC more frequently — daily during heat waves — and be ready to dose more often. The FC/CYA targets in the table above apply at typical pool temperatures; in very warm water, staying toward the top of your target range is safer than the bottom.

How do I raise pool chlorine levels quickly?

The fastest way to raise FC is liquid chlorine (sodium hypochlorite, 10–12.5%). It dissolves instantly, raises FC within minutes of adding with the pump running, and doesn't add CYA — so it won't push your stabilizer higher over time.

To calculate how much to add: you need your pool volume, current FC, and target FC. As a rough guide, 1 fl oz of 12.5% liquid chlorine raises FC by roughly 1 ppm per 1,000 gallons. For a 15,000-gallon pool that needs FC raised from 1 to 4 ppm, that's about 45 fl oz (just under 1.5 quarts).
