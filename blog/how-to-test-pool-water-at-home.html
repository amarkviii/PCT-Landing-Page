How to test pool water at home: step-by-step

9 min read · Updated June 5, 2026

Testing your pool water is the single most important habit in pool care. Every chemical decision you make — how much chlorine to add, whether to adjust pH, when to shock — depends on knowing your current numbers. Guessing leads to problems. Testing takes five minutes.

Here's how to do it right, what to test for, and how to avoid the common mistakes that give you bad readings.

What you'll need

You have two main options for home testing. Both work — they trade off convenience against accuracy.

1

Test Strips Quick & Easy

How they work: Dip a strip in pool water, wait 15–30 seconds, compare the color pads to the chart on the bottle.

Pros: Fast, cheap, no measuring. Good for quick daily or mid-week checks.

Cons: Less precise — you're eyeballing colors, and lighting matters. Harder to read exact values for parameters like alkalinity. Strips expire and degrade if exposed to moisture.

Best for: Chlorine and pH spot checks between full tests.

2

Liquid Test Kit More Accurate

How they work: Fill a sample vial with pool water, add drops of reagent, count drops or compare colors. The most popular home kit uses the DPD method for chlorine and phenol red for pH.

Pros: Much more accurate than strips, especially for chlorine and pH. The FAS-DPD drop-count method gives you a precise chlorine number, not a color range.

Cons: Takes longer (2–3 minutes per test), reagents expire annually, slightly more expensive upfront.

Best for: Your primary 2–3x per week testing. Worth the investment.

Most experienced pool owners use a liquid kit for their regular tests and keep strips around for quick sanity checks. For a deeper comparison, see test strips vs liquid test kits.

How to collect a good sample

A bad sample gives you bad numbers. Here's how to get a reading that actually reflects your pool:

1

Collect from elbow depth

Reach your arm in (elbow deep — about 12–18 inches below the surface) and collect the sample there. Surface water is exposed to sunlight and air, so chlorine and pH readings at the surface don't represent the rest of the pool.

2

Away from returns and skimmers

Don't sample near a return jet (you'll get freshly treated water) or the skimmer (you'll get surface water being pulled in). Collect from the middle of the pool or at least a few feet from any jets.

3

Pump should be running

Test after the pump has been running for at least 30 minutes. This ensures chemicals are circulated and you're reading the actual pool chemistry, not a stagnant pocket.

4

Rinse your vial first

If using a liquid test kit, rinse the sample vial with pool water before filling it. Residual reagent or tap water from the last use will skew your results.

Step-by-step: testing with strips

  1. Remove one strip from the bottle and close the cap immediately — moisture degrades the remaining strips
  2. Dip the strip into the pool (elbow depth) and swirl gently for 2 seconds
  3. Remove and hold horizontally — don't shake off excess water
  4. Wait the time specified on the bottle (usually 15–30 seconds)
  5. Compare each pad to the color chart in good lighting — natural daylight is best
  6. Read within the time window — colors keep changing after a few minutes and become unreliable

Don't touch the pads

Oil from your fingers affects the reagent chemistry. Hold strips by the end or edges only.

Step-by-step: testing with a liquid kit

  1. Rinse the sample vial with pool water, then fill to the marked line
  2. Add the specified number of reagent drops — hold the dropper bottle vertically for consistent drop size
  3. Cap and invert gently to mix (don't shake vigorously — bubbles interfere with color reads)
  4. Compare the color to the chart, or count drops until color change (for FAS-DPD chlorine test)
  5. Read the result immediately — don't let the sample sit
  6. Rinse the vial thoroughly with tap water after each test

The FAS-DPD advantage

Standard DPD color-match tests max out around 5 ppm chlorine and can bleach out at high levels (showing clear instead of dark pink — which makes you think chlorine is zero when it's actually very high). The FAS-DPD drop-count method works at any chlorine level and gives you an exact number. If you're buying a kit, get one with FAS-DPD.

When to test (and when not to)

Best time to test: Morning or early evening, when the sun isn't at peak intensity. Chlorine levels drop throughout the day as UV breaks it down, so a mid-afternoon reading will be your daily low point. Read more in our guide on the best time to test pool water.

Don't test right after:

  • Adding chemicals — wait at least 30 minutes with the pump running (longer for CYA and calcium, which dissolve slowly)
  • Heavy rain — rain dilutes surface water and changes pH temporarily. Run the pump for an hour first
  • A pool party — swimmers introduce contaminants that take time to circulate and be oxidized. Test the next morning instead
  • Shocking — wait until chlorine drops back below 5 ppm before testing for your regular baseline

Common testing mistakes

  • Using expired reagents or strips — reagents lose accuracy after about a year. Check the expiration date. If your results seem off, this is the most likely reason.
  • Testing at the surface — surface water has different chemistry than the body of the pool. Always go elbow deep.
  • Testing near a return jet — freshly circulated water near the jets may have different chemical levels than the main body.
  • Not rinsing the vial — leftover reagent from the last test contaminates the new sample.
  • Reading strips too late — colors keep developing after the time window. Read them when the bottle says to, not five minutes later.
  • Storing strips in the bathroom or pool shed — humidity degrades them. Keep the bottle sealed, in a cool dry place.

What about pool store testing?

Many pool stores offer free water testing. It's worth doing occasionally — their equipment is more precise than home kits for parameters like CYA and calcium hardness. But don't rely on it as your only testing method.

Pool store tests are a snapshot from when you collected the sample (which may have been sitting in a hot car for an hour). They're also designed to sell you chemicals. Some stores' recommendations are solid; others will try to sell you products you don't need. If you know your own numbers, you can evaluate their advice instead of following it blindly.

Bring a clean sample

If you do take water to a pool store, collect it the same way — elbow deep, away from jets, pump running. Use a clean container (rinse it with pool water first, not tap water). Take it straight to the store — don't let it sit in the sun or a hot car for hours.

Recording your results

Testing is only half the job. If you don't write down the numbers, you can't spot trends — and trends are how you catch problems early. A steady downward drift in chlorine tells you something different than a sudden crash. Rising calcium over months means your source water is hard. Patterns matter.

You can use a notebook, a spreadsheet, or an app — the format doesn't matter as long as you actually do it every time you test. Learn the 5 key numbers if you're just getting started.

Common questions

How often should you test pool water?

Free chlorine and pH: 2–3 times per week during swimming season — they change fastest and matter most for safety. Total alkalinity: weekly. Calcium hardness and CYA: monthly. After heavy rain, a pool party, or any chemical addition, test the next morning before swimming.

What is the best time of day to test pool water?

Morning or early evening. Chlorine drops throughout the day as UV burns it off — a mid-afternoon reading shows your daily low and can look alarming even when maintenance is fine. Morning gives you a stable baseline.

Are test strips accurate enough?

Accurate enough for chlorine and pH spot checks. For alkalinity, calcium hardness, and CYA, a liquid kit is significantly more precise — you're matching colors visually with strips, and lighting affects the result. Most pool owners use strips for quick daily checks and a liquid kit for their full 2–3x/week tests.

Where should you collect the water sample?

Elbow depth (12–18 inches below the surface), away from return jets and the skimmer, with the pump running for at least 30 minutes. Surface water and water near jets don't represent the actual pool chemistry.

How long after adding chemicals should you wait to test?

At least 30 minutes with the pump running for most chemicals. For CYA (stabilizer), wait 2–3 days — it dissolves slowly and won't register accurately until fully circulated. Never add a second dose before retesting.

What should I test my pool water for?

Five core parameters: free chlorine (2–4 ppm, adjusted for CYA), pH (7.4–7.8), total alkalinity (60–90 ppm), calcium hardness (200–400 ppm), and CYA (30–50 ppm). Also test total chlorine so you can calculate combined chlorine (TC − FC). Free chlorine and pH are the most time-sensitive — check those most often.
