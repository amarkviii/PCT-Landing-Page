Pool chlorine guides

Free chlorine (FC) is the front line of water safety, but getting it right is more nuanced than just adding liquid and hoping. FC disappears faster in hot weather, your CYA level sets the minimum you actually need, and combined chlorine — the gap between TC and FC — tells you when there's a problem chlorine alone won't fix. These guides cover the full picture: how to read your numbers, why FC vanishes overnight, which chlorine type to use, and the FC/CYA relationship that determines your real target.

Understanding pool chlorine

Free chlorine (FC) is the only form that sanitizes — it's the chlorine ready to react with bacteria, algae, and organic contaminants on contact. When FC does its job and gets used up, it becomes combined chlorine (CC): spent chlorine that still registers on a test kit but no longer protects your pool. Total chlorine (TC) = FC + CC. The gap between TC and FC is your early warning number — keep it below 0.5 ppm and your sanitizer is working efficiently. A gap above 0.5 ppm means chloramines are accumulating and a shock dose is needed.

Your FC target isn't a flat 2–4 ppm — it's a percentage of your CYA (cyanuric acid) level. CYA stabilizes chlorine against UV loss, but at higher concentrations it also reduces the active fraction of FC. The practical rule: FC minimum = CYA × 7.5% (or × 5% for saltwater pools). A pool with CYA 50 ppm needs FC above 3.75 ppm; a pool with CYA 80 ppm needs FC above 6 ppm. The FC/CYA chart translates this into a ready reference for any stabilizer level.

Common chlorine problems follow predictable patterns. FC dropping overnight: high demand from heat, bather load, organic debris, or CYA that's too low. CC above 0.5 ppm: chloramines accumulating — shock to breakpoint chlorination to clear them. Chlorine that won't hold after adding product: usually a CYA problem or invisible early-stage algae. The guides below cover each scenario with the specific diagnosis and fix.
