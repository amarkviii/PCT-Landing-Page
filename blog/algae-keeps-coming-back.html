Why algae keeps coming back in your pool (and how to stop it)

7 min read · August 5, 2026 · By Jeffrey Thompson

Algae keeps coming back when the root cause isn't fixed. The most common reasons: free chlorine regularly dips below the minimum for your CYA level, CYA has crept high enough that normal doses of chlorine can't maintain an effective concentration, or the previous treatment was stopped before the algae was completely gone. Fixing recurring algae means finding the pattern — which requires tracking readings over time.

The pattern that identifies the cause

A pool that cycles between "clear" and "green" every few weeks almost always has one of the five causes below. You can diagnose which one by looking at your reading history: when does FC drop below the CYA-based minimum? How fast does it drop? Has CYA been rising over the season? Without a log of readings, you're guessing. With a log, the answer is usually obvious.

Cause 1: FC regularly drops below the minimum for your CYA

Most common cause

FC drops below the algae threshold between tests

The FC/CYA relationship sets a hard minimum below which algae can establish. At CYA 50 ppm, the minimum FC is about 3.75 ppm (50 × 7.5%). If FC drops below this — even briefly, even overnight — algae spores that are always present in pool water begin to multiply. A pool that's "fine when I test it" but runs below the minimum for 18 hours a day can develop a bloom in days.

The most common triggers for dips: a heavy weekend of swimming (bather load depletes FC), a hot week with strong UV (accelerates FC consumption), a few days without adding chlorine, or running low on supply.

Fix: Use the FC/CYA chart to find your actual minimum, not a generic "aim for 3 ppm" number. Test more frequently — 3+ times per week during swim season. Add chlorine before FC drops, not after.

Cause 2: CYA is too high

Second most common cause

CYA above 60–80 ppm traps chlorine

CYA stabilizes chlorine against UV degradation, but at high concentrations it also reduces the active fraction of chlorine available for sanitation. At CYA 80 ppm, the minimum FC to prevent algae is 6 ppm — and many pool owners maintaining "normal" FC of 2–3 ppm are chronically below that threshold without realizing it.

CYA rises over the season if you use trichlor tablets (each ppm of FC raised with trichlor adds about 0.6 ppm CYA) or dichlor granules. By mid-summer, a pool started at CYA 40 ppm in May might be at CYA 90+ ppm in August — and every chlorine dose now works at a fraction of its apparent effectiveness.

Fix: Test CYA monthly. If CYA exceeds 60–70 ppm, do a partial drain and refill to bring it into the 30–50 ppm range. Use the CYA dilution calculator to find how much to drain. Switch to liquid chlorine as your primary sanitizer to stop CYA from rising further.

Cause 3: Treatment was stopped too early

Very common with DIY treatments

Algae survived at low concentration and regrew

A pool that looks clear after shocking isn't necessarily algae-free. Algae can be reduced to a concentration too low to be visible while still alive. Once FC returns to maintenance levels, the surviving cells multiply and the bloom returns — often appearing as green or hazy water within 3–7 days of the "successful" treatment.

This is the reason the overnight chlorine loss test (OCLT) exists. Living algae in the water consumes FC measurably overnight even at low concentrations. A passing OCLT (FC drops 1 ppm or less from dusk to dawn with the pump off) is reliable confirmation that the algae is actually gone. Stopping when the water "looks clear" without running the OCLT is stopping early.

Fix: Always run the OCLT before returning to maintenance mode. See the OCLT calculator for an automatic pass/fail verdict. If it fails, keep shocking.

Cause 4: Algae survived on equipment, toys, or swimsuits

The main cause of mustard algae recurrence

Spores reenter the pool after treatment

This cause specifically applies to mustard algae, which survives on fabric and equipment surfaces. The pool clears perfectly after treatment, and then the first swim in an unwashed suit reintroduces the spores. The algae re-establishes within days in the same shaded spots it occupied before.

Pool toys, floats, kick boards, vacuum heads, and brush heads can also carry spores between treatments. If mustard algae has appeared in your pool twice, and you didn't wash your swimsuits after the first incident, this is almost certainly the cause of the return.

Fix: After treating mustard algae, wash all swimsuits in hot water and dry on high heat. Soak all pool equipment in a dilute bleach solution (1 tbsp liquid chlorine per gallon of water) for 15 minutes, then rinse. Clean filter media thoroughly before returning to maintenance mode.

Cause 5: Dead spots from poor circulation

Often overlooked

Algae establishes in areas the pump doesn't reach

Pool circulation is rarely uniform. Corners behind steps, the middle of the deep end floor, and areas far from return jets can have significantly lower FC and chlorine contact than the rest of the pool. Algae establishes in these pockets first and spreads outward. If algae always appears in the same corner or area of your pool, poor circulation is likely a contributing factor.

Indicators of a dead-spot problem: algae consistently starts in one location, brushing that area reveals heavier algae growth than elsewhere, and the area feels slimy or has a different texture.

Fix: Redirect return jets to create better circulation in problem areas. Consider adding a circulation booster or checking that all jets are pointing at the right angle. Run the pump for more hours per day in hot weather. Brush dead spots daily during the swim season.

How to diagnose which cause you have

Run through this diagnostic in order:

QuestionIf yes, the likely cause is…
Is your CYA above 70 ppm?Cause 2 (high CYA). Lower it before anything else.
Do you use trichlor tablets as your primary sanitizer?Cause 2 (rising CYA). Switch to liquid chlorine, test CYA monthly.
Did you run the OCLT after your last treatment?If no: Cause 3. Always run the OCLT. If yes and it passed, move to the next question.
Is the algae mustard (yellow-brown, in shaded spots, brushes off)?Cause 4. Decontaminate swimsuits and equipment before the next swim.
Does algae always start in the same corner or area?Cause 5. Circulation dead spot. Redirect returns and brush that area daily.
Does your FC match the CYA minimum between tests?Cause 1. Increase testing frequency and adjust dosing routine.

Why tracking readings finds the pattern

The most powerful diagnostic tool for recurring algae is a log of your readings over time. With a record of FC, CYA, and the dates of each test, you can answer questions that are impossible to answer from memory:

  • How low does FC actually get between tests? (Is it regularly dipping below the minimum?)
  • Has CYA been rising every month? (Steady creep from tablet use)
  • How many days after a shock treatment did FC return to maintenance level vs. when algae appeared?
  • Did the last treatment actually pass the OCLT?

Without a reading history, each algae bloom looks like a fresh problem. With a history, you can see that FC has been hitting 1.5 ppm every weekend for six weeks and algae blooms on Tuesday. That's not a mystery; it's a predictable pattern with a fixable cause.

The one-time fix checklist

If algae has been a recurring problem this season, work through this list once and it should stop:

  1. Test CYA. If above 60–70 ppm, do a partial drain now. Don't skip this step — no maintenance routine compensates for CYA above 80.
  2. Shock the pool and run the OCLT. Treat current algae completely before fixing the prevention side.
  3. Identify your true FC minimum from the FC/CYA chart and set it as your target floor, not your generic dosing number.
  4. Switch to liquid chlorine if you currently use tablets as your primary sanitizer, so CYA stays stable going forward.
  5. Test FC 3× per week minimum and add chlorine proactively before FC approaches the minimum.
  6. Brush weekly — especially shaded areas, corners, and the waterline where algae first colonizes.

Track the pattern. Stop treating symptoms.
